2046. Every job has a robot built for it.

Not one robot that tries to do everything. Thousands of specialists, each shaped by the task it was made for.

Towers

Crawlers clean every facade. Gantries print new floors overnight.

Port and streets

Cranes, sorters and street bots, each built for one job.

Rooftops and bridges

Rooftop farms picked by arms. Bridge cables inspected by snakes.

None of these robots was designed by hand. A startup built the process that makes them.

Thesis · the world today

Today, every robot is a custom project that starts from zero.

The human body is one possible design, and it has limits. A general-purpose humanoid can match human capability. A machine shaped by its task can exceed it. The question is how far automation can go once building that machine is easy.

What if a robot for any task could be designed, trained and built on request?

Overview

We propose a generalized process for creating task-specific robots.

Given details on a specific task, we iterate over various modular combinations in order to determine which robot design performs the best. Then, we use reinforcement learning in order to create a specialized model for the task. A hybrid pipeline of mass production and skilled human assembly then creates the final robot.

Despite limitations of task-specific robots when compared to generalized designs, we achieve positive unit-economics as well as sustainability through a contract model and reusable components. Ultimately, we hope to lessen the barrier to entry for manufacturing, reinvigorating domestic manufacturing and innovation.

The four BASES questions · our answers

Q1

Will sim-to-real be solved, or sample efficiency get high enough to train policies quickly on little data? What then happens to companies built to sell robot data?

Sim-to-real will be solved, and our database of actuator and robot capabilities mapped to tasks keeps the data needed small. Robot data companies will either fall or adapt into robot data generation pipelines.

Q2

What form factors will robot data companies use, which labor markets will the data come from, and what export controls will appear?

They will turn into simulation farms, or post-training contractors that we contract with, and data collection moves to wearable technology in daily life.

Q3

Humanoids or task-specific machines: which wins in homes, warehouses and factories, and why?

Not a binary question. Humanoids win in homes. Task-specific machines win in small businesses and bulk manufacturing.

Q4

Which job will robots take over first at scale, and what do the unit economics look like against human labor?

Manufacturing first. Task-specific robots built cheaply but robustly from a shared parts library make the unit economics really cheap.

Simulation · answers Q1

For each customer task, we design a world simulation that has parameters customized for the task.

We set precise definitions of the task at hand, and determine site constraints (ceiling heights, power access, where people walk).

As described in our technical details section below, we believe we can ensure accuracy in the training environment, measuring friction, stiffness, fragility of items, etc. We also find benchmarks within the real world and see where our robots can best optimize.

Q1

Sim-to-real will be solved. World models generate 3D worlds from prompts, RL with physics engines like MuJoCo simulates tons of data, smarter models need less data, and growing compute makes custom worlds and accurate physics affordable.

RL Framework · answers Q1

In our simulation, we begin by evaluating a “parent configuration,” a well-understood robot design that best matches the requisite task.

From there, we evaluate hundreds of modularized variants (candidate configurations) from our parts library that can interact with the customer’s task.

Before we run any specific simulations, we utilize a physical constraint model to reject designs that exceed torque, payload, connector load limits, etc.

Q1

Companies built to sell robot data will either fall or adapt, moving into robot data generation pipelines: custom prompt or world engineering, efficient pipeline development for RL engineers, and labs for novel task generation.

RL Framework · answers Q2

For each finalist, a model writes a reward function suitable for that variant’s structure.

The control policies of the finalists are refined through thousands of randomized simulations involving the requisite task. Finalists are ranked on specific criteria chosen by the customer (e.g. task success, cycle time, dollar cost) to find the winner.

Real-world results from each deployment are fed back into our database and module calibrations, so we can make better future recommendations with our model.

Q2

Robot data companies will turn into simulation farms, or into post-training contractors that our startup contracts with. Data collection comes from wearable technology in daily life.

Robot Framework · answers Q3

Our design is highly modular, so once our RL framework settles on the best arrangement of components, we simply put the pieces together.

Any of our designs can be made from combinations of core components, joints, structural links, locomotive devices, end effectors, and sensors, all of which can be linked together with a universal connector.

This enables a broad range of capabilities, both from the same manufacturing process and from the same instantiated robot.

Q3

It is not a binary question. Humanoids win in homes. Task-specific machines win in small businesses and bulk manufacturing, because a modular body shaped by the task exceeds human capability instead of copying it.

Manufacturing · answers Q4

We mass-produce components for our project, and we would aim to own the entire vertical stack when it comes to these mechanical components.

Our core techniques are CNC machining, injection molding, and PCB assembly. Parts of this process are highly automatable; in other parts, though, it truly is more efficient to hire skilled, human labor.

To assemble our robots, we simply join these components with universal connectors. This is highly automatable.

Q4

Unit economics are really cheap: task-specific robots are optimized for cheap but robust construction, and our parts and actuators library means cheap components at scale.

Economics · answers Q4

Despite the typical disadvantages of task-specific hardware when compared to generalized approaches, we achieve positive unit economics through our business model.

Our biggest markets would be specialized tasks in manufacturing, agriculture, and warehouse automation, but we also see significant use-cases for small businesses.

We are contracted with companies, charge a fee when implementing a new robot design, and implement a subscription model to charge for new instances and to lease the models’ components. We achieve economies of scale by reusing components for new applications. In doing so, we also maintain sustainability and circumvent the scarcity of the requisite rare earth magnets.

Q4

Robots will take over manufacturing first, then agriculture and warehouse automation, with small businesses close behind.

Impact

Our model will dramatically boost manufacturing capability, especially domestically.

This enables the country, for the first time regarding matters that are not related to national security, to circumvent the recent crisis regarding cost of labor in the US. Our priorities include replacing or assisting with dangerous manufacturing and first-response jobs.

Ultimately, we also believe that we will enable new innovations. Since humanoid robots simply match human capabilities, while our robots exceed human capabilities, we aim to push the frontier of what is possible through engineering.

In terms of scale, we also foresee a recursive element. Implementing our own robots in order to optimize our manufacturing process will improve our efficiency.

Technical details · 1 of 4

How can we predict the behavior of a newly assembled robot?

Known components make the modeling problem smaller. We reuse their physical descriptions, then calibrate the interactions introduced by the new assembly.

Technical details: The assembly graph determines geometry, mass distribution, and joint connections. Component models supply actuator behavior; short calibration routines estimate remaining errors from friction, connector flexibility, and tolerances. Stronger AI could automate experiment selection and model correction.

Research: Agile legged locomotion in reconfigurable modular robots — PNAS, 2026 demonstrates physical modular assemblies with learned control.

Technical details · 2 of 4

Does every robot body in a new environment need to learn from scratch?

Changing the body does not erase all useful knowledge. A controller can reuse skills while adapting to the new arrangement.

Technical details: Condition the policy on an assembly graph containing module properties and connectivity. Share parameters across compatible modules, pretrain across many simulated configurations, and fine-tune unfamiliar assemblies. Initially restrict transfer claims to a defined component and task family.

Research: Learning Modular Robot Control Policies — IEEE Transactions on Robotics, 2023 demonstrates shared policies and generalization to unseen modular designs.

Technical details · 3 of 4

How can we find good designs of new robots at a low cost?

The system learns which designs deserve expensive testing. Experience from earlier projects narrows the search for the next one.

Technical details: Reject physically infeasible assemblies first, screen candidates with inexpensive evaluations, then allocate longer training to promising or uncertain designs. Evaluate learning curves so a potentially superior body is not rejected merely because it learns slowly. RL trains behavior; design search can use RL, Bayesian optimization, or evolutionary methods.

Research: Transform2Act — ICLR, 2022 demonstrates experience sharing across design and control. This is a conference paper.

Technical details · 4 of 4

Why should a robot optimized in simulation work in reality?

Simulation needs to capture the variations that affect successful control. Robust policies can tolerate some modeling error, with physical testing identifying consequential mismatches.

Technical details: Vary friction, contact, actuator response, sensor noise, and delay during training. Give the deployed policy only measurements its actual sensors provide. Test shortlisted designs under held-out conditions and physically validate them; use calibration or targeted post-training when transfer falls short.

Research: IndustReal — RSS, 2023 demonstrates bounded transfer for contact-rich assembly. It supports this mechanism, not universal sim-to-real transfer.
